# 02 — Seed-to-Soil Journey and Stakeholders

﻿SEED-TO-SOIL FOOD SYSTEM JOURNEY & STAKEHOLDER MAP
AI Builder Cup — Sustainability & Social Impact | Version 0.1 | 14 October 2026
Scope: India metro-first observation, JAPAC comparators later; map hypotheses separately from measured outcomes. Not a product plan.


1. DEFINITIONS AND BOUNDARIES
Food LOSS: maturity/harvesting through storage, processing, wholesale, excluding retail (FAO SDG 12.3.1a). Food WASTE: retail, food service and households (UNEP SDG 12.3.1b). Separate edible/avoidable material from inedible scraps. “Surplus” remains potentially useful; not every surplus item is safe or suitable for donation. Once waste is generated, diversion (feed, compost, digestion) is different from prevention. Do NOT add FAO percentages to UNEP tonnage.


2. COMPLETE FOOD JOURNEY — STAGE-BY-STAGE
01 Seed and farm planning | People: growers, families, input sellers, cooperatives, agricultural advisers. Decisions: crop, planting timing, seed and irrigation plans, expected buyer. Potential loss: wrong supply/timing, weather shock, rejected produce. Substitutes: cooperative buying, extension services, contracts. AI fit: local-language document/context synthesis; conventional forecasting usually central. Major unknown: who can actually change demand or weather exposure.


02 Cultivation | People: farmers, farm workers, irrigation and input suppliers. Decisions: fertilizer, irrigation, pest response. Loss: crop failures, water and pesticide burdens, contamination. Substitutes: agronomists and advisory services. AI fit: image/voice observations only if validated. Physical action and agronomic risk dominate.


03 Harvest and grading | People: harvest workers, aggregators, graders, traders. Decisions: harvest time, quality specifications, reject/resell. Loss: bruising, oversupply and cosmetic standards. Alternatives: buyer standards, secondary markets. AI role: interpreting grading evidence and changing orders; strong need for reliable images and labels.


04 Collection, transport, cold chain | People: transporters, cold-store managers, market agents, loaders. Decisions: temperature, route, packing, speed, transfer. Loss: spoilage and damage. Alternatives: reefer monitoring, IoT, cold rooms. AI role: operational records, claim reconciliation; sensors and infrastructure central.


05 Processing and wholesale | People: factories, millers, processors, wholesale markets. Decisions: batches, specifications, shelf life, outlet. Loss: trimming, overruns, unsold stock. Alternatives: ERP, inventory and predictive planning. AI role: exception/context interpretation; requires proprietary data.


06 Retail, kirana, supermarket | People: store owners, category managers, checkout staff, buyers. Decisions: purchase quantities, shelf rotation, markdowns, assortment. Loss: unsold, damaged, expired goods; theft and shrink are distinct. Alternatives: POS/inventory/markdown tools, food banks. AI role: mixed records and explanations; replenishment forecasting typically rules/ML.


07 Quick commerce / dark store | People: platform merchandising, sellers, warehouse pickers, delivery partners, consumers. Decisions: small rapid orders, minimum quantities, substitutes, promotions, fulfillment, freshness. Loss points HYPOTHESES: late-dated food, repeated top-ups, promotions encouraging unnecessary volume, assortment mismatch, damaged returns. No direct causal evidence established that quick commerce increases consumer food waste. Platform operational inventory and order history largely private. Technical pathways: authorized partner API only; screenshots/manual user-supplied orders for consented research; no unauthorized scraping/auto-purchase. Competing companies: Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart, BigBasket/bbnow, JioMart, Flipkart Minutes. India market evidence: Redseer reported approx six q-commerce orders per transacting user per month and ~90% metro share in FY24; figures are not unique household frequency.


08 Restaurants and food delivery | People: restaurant owners, chefs, aggregators, couriers, eaters. Decisions: menu, ingredient purchasing, prep timing, portions, delivery packaging, promotions. Loss points: overproduction, order cancellations, customer leftovers, spoilage, returned orders. Competing tools: Swiggy/Zomato operations, Winnow/Leanpath, POS. Avoid the false assumption that ordering delivery always increases waste; depends on substitution for cooking, meal size, packaging and delivery.


09 Home purchase and storage | People: adult household shopper(s), partner, grandparents, children, house help, cook, roommates. Decisions: weekly stock-up, daily milk/vegetables, immediate quick commerce, grocery substitutions, what is already at home, storage. Loss points: duplicate purchases, poor storage, forgotten perishables, mismatched pack sizes. Delhi 56-household physical audit found 0.749 kg avoidable waste per household per week over seven days and implicated coordination, overcooking and changing work/social events. THIS is a small nonrepresentative study.


10 Household meal planning and cooking | People: whoever cooks (often not same as buyer), meal participants, caretakers, delivery app users. Decisions: who eats at home today, portions, ingredients to use, dietary needs, takeout vs cooking. Loss: leftovers, unused prepared food, unsafe handling. Alternatives: shared calendars, Samsung Food, NoWaste, family messages, domestic routines, conventional portion planning. Gap question: when circumstances change, does a time-sensitive decision remain unsolved after these tools?


11 Shared/community/institutional kitchens | People: schools, colleges, hostels, university dining, office cafeterias, hospitals, nursing homes, old-age homes, orphanage kitchen managers, caterers, workers, students, patients, residents. Decisions: actual attendance, dietary restrictions, batch sizes, buffet restocking, portions, procurement. Loss: preparation surplus, serving-line waste, plate waste. Separate safe, untouched surplus from served leftovers; do not automatically treat old-age or orphanage institutions as recipients of random donated meals. Schools and care facilities have specific nutrition/food-safety obligations.


12 Surplus recovery and redistribution | People: donors (households/restaurants/retailers), FSSAI-compliant recovery organizations, volunteers, food banks, vetted receiving institutions, recipients. Decisions: identity, quantity, packaging, hygiene, temperature, time, pickup route, recipient acceptance, documentation, traceability. Failure hypotheses: stale postings, impractical pickup distance, insufficient cold chain, mismatched dietary needs, lack of verified custody. Regulations in India define eligible surplus as safe, unused, not served to customers; different categories require appropriate safe handling. FSSAI IFSA already includes agencies, e.g. Robin Hood Army and Feeding India. No unsupervised matching or declaration of safe food from photos.


13 Non-human or industrial recovery | People: suitable animal-feed processors, compost operators, anaerobic digesters, municipalities, segregators, agricultural users. Decisions: safe classification, separation, contamination controls, collection frequency. Destinations: regulated animal feed (where lawful), industrial upcycling, compost, anaerobic digestion, then lower-preference disposal. Use EPA Wasted Food Scale for conceptual precedence; local law controls specifics.


14 Final disposal | People: waste collection teams, municipal contractors, landfill operators, households, businesses. Outcomes: contaminated mixed waste, landfill methane, waste-to-energy and incineration with varied impacts, wastewater disposal. Measure what physically goes where; never infer all diverted food avoids landfill without collection records.


3. STAKEHOLDER JOURNEYS AND JOBS TO BE DONE
A Metro multi-generation family | Shopper: adult; cook: parent/domestic worker; children: school lunch; older resident: specific diet; office worker: lunch elsewhere; multiple independent orderers. Jobs: avoid duplicate items; balance nutrition and appetite; adjust to guests, late shifts, eating out. Direct outcomes: edible kg discarded; household spend; labor/time; satisfaction. Biggest risk: increased data entry and unwanted monitoring of household members.


B Flatmates / working professionals | Different schedules, spontaneous restaurant ordering, shared vs personal food, fridge ownership. Jobs: resolve shared stock and meal participation with consent. Potential outcome: fewer spoiled perishables; compare to ordinary shared list/chat.


C Single-person urban household | Variable meals, package sizes, limited cooking frequency. Distinct problem from multi-person coordination: flexible portions, spoilage, preservation. Alternative: buying smaller packs and simpler shopping habits.


D Quick-commerce shopper | Top-up purchase via Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart; compares price/quality/quantity and offers; purchases can be spontaneous. Research whether offer-led purchasing correlates with unused groceries; evidence UNKNOWN. Do not invent ability to automate a checkout or access private accounts; any purchase would require authentication, partnership and explicit approval.


E Delivery app customer | Orders prepared meals from Swiggy/Zomato or local restaurant; portions and leftovers vary. Potential measurement: purchased portions vs consumed vs discarded; packaging count. Use Swiggy group-ordering incumbent in competitor analysis.


F Kirana/supermarket owner | Tracks stock, sell-by windows, margins, waste, donations. May benefit from a B2B2C partnership; proof requires inventory/expiry and actual waste/markdown records.


G Quick-commerce dark-store manager | Manages near-real-time replenishment, supplier shelf life, stockouts and losses. High scale but private data and mature internal optimization; test independently before advancing.


H School/college/hostel caterer | Counts enrolled/expected eaters, absences, menus, serving, plate leftovers. Distinguish kitchen overproduction from plate waste; can be weighed. Competes with Leanpath/Winnow and simple attendance-aware planning.


I Office cafeteria / hotel buffet | Demand varies by day, events, remote work, guests. Established enterprise competition; evaluate smaller-budget operators not served by heavyweight solutions.


J Hospital/elder-care/orphanage food service | Menus, medical/special diets, safety, procurement, plate waste. Protected populations: do not propose donations without verified nutrition suitability, safety and organization consent. Physical collection/food service systems may dominate.


K Restaurant chef/owner | Orders ingredients and prepares dishes; surplus can be safe but time-limited. Competes with POS, forecast, discounted-surplus apps and food rescue.


L NGO / community kitchen | Needs safe inbound supply, capacity, transport, storage and actual demand. Recipient institutions must control acceptance; donor supply does not equal hunger reduction.


M Waste worker and processor | Receives food material after prevention; safety, segregation and viable compost/feed market are primary constraints.


4. QUICK-COMMERCE RESEARCH LENS: INDIA METRO FIRST
Suggested pilot geography: choose ONE metro later, e.g. Bengaluru, Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Hyderabad or Pune, based on access to consenting households and actual ordering patterns. Metro-first is defensible because Redseer estimated ~90% of Indian q-commerce GMV in metros FY24; extrapolation to Tier 2/3 would require new evidence. Track all channels: weekly supermarket, kirana, informal market, quick commerce, restaurant delivery, subscriptions/tiffin, gifts, office/school meals. Do not equate app frequency with edible-food waste.


Hypothetical household week (not survey evidence): grandparent shops market twice, parent orders grocery stock-up once, adult orders 2 quick-commerce top-ups, another adult buys lunch outside three days, cook prepares five dinners, student eats school lunches. Independent orders produce a possible mismatch; quantities, frequency, behavior and waste must be observed rather than assumed.


Consent-safe research artifacts: manually entered order emails/receipts; de-identified screenshots uploaded by owner; grocery invoices; meal calendar; aggregate food-weight log. Never demand account passwords or scrape behind login. Actual ordering needs explicit user confirmation and authorized merchant integrations.


5. POST-WASTE DECISION BOUNDARIES
Not every leftover is donation-eligible. Food handled/served to individuals, temperature abuse, allergens, uncertain provenance or unsuitable diets require rejection from edible recovery. EPA pathways, subject to local law: prevent > donate/upcycle safe edible surplus > lawful animal feed > compost/beneficial digestion > landfill/incineration/sewer. Donation does not necessarily reduce hunger unless safe food reaches and is consumed by people who need it.


6. CROSS-CUTTING METRICS, CAUSAL ACCOUNTING
Source loss: kg lost / kg harvested, per commodity and stage.
Retail waste: kg unsold spoilage / kg received; markdowns actually sold.
Household: kg avoidable edible discarded / household-week; count of duplicate purchases; food spending, net after behavior.
Food service: kitchen excess/plate leftovers kg per meal served; meal attendance.
Recovery: safe kg accepted/delivered/consumed, rejected kg and reasons, time-temperature compliance.
Environmental: food-specific embodied energy/water and CO2e factors, avoided production only when substitution is plausible, disposal methane separately; deduct extra collection/AI/transport footprint. Social: time saved, cost, meal access, nutrition suitability, food safety.


7. FIRST-PASS RESEARCH STATUS AND REASONS TO PARK
Advance to falsification: multi-person household change events (F01); pre/post meal attendance mismatch in small canteens (F03); time-sensitive safe surplus handoffs (F05).
Narrow: quick-commerce order fragmentation (F02) — no independent causal waste link yet; retail/dark-store losses (F04) — private data; plate-vs-kitchen waste (F03) — separate mechanisms.
Defer: citywide universal food redistribution marketplace — FSSAI/NGOs already exist, cold chain/safety/logistics; all-in-one farm-to-table product — too many owners and data silos; automatic cross-app purchasing — partner APIs/auth and consent unresolved; end-stage compost alone — less impact than prevention when otherwise edible food exists.
